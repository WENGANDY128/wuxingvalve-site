Technical Extension: From Phase Diagram to Process—How Three "Critical Lines" Guide Valve Production

2026-06-26 Heat Treatment Phase Diagram / Valve Manufacturing / Failure Analysis

The core value of the iron-carbon phase diagram lies in marking three critical temperature lines that determine microstructure transformation. However, the engineering application of these three lines is far more complex than simply "heating up to a certain temperature."

1. A1 Line (Eutectoid Line, 727°C): The Temperature "Anchor" of Heat Treatment Processes

The A1 line represents the boundary of mutual transformation between pearlite and austenite, serving as the baseline temperature for all valve heat treatment processes. In actual production, due to the lag effect in heating and cooling, the phase transformation points shift:

  • During heating, the actual transformation point is denoted as Ac1 ("c" for chauffage/heating), which is always higher than the equilibrium A1 point.
  • During cooling, the actual transformation point is denoted as Ar1 ("r" for refroidissement/cooling), which is always lower than the equilibrium A1 point.

Engineering Significance: The faster the heating rate, the more Ac1 deviates from the equilibrium point. This means that when quenching or fully normalizing hypo-eutectoid forged steel valves such as ASTM A105, crossing Ac1 only transforms pearlite to austenite. The theoretical value of 727°C cannot be mechanically applied as the final heating target; instead, the material must be further heated to 30–50°C above Ac3 to determine the actual holding temperature.

If the heating temperature is too low (failing to effectively cross Ac3 to reach the fully austenitic zone), the pro-eutectoid ferrite in the original structure will not dissolve completely. Consequently, undissolved ferrite will appear in the matrix after quenching, leading to uneven hardness. This has provided clear lessons in past failure cases of steam turbine control valve stems: excessively high ferrite content directly caused the material strength and impact toughness to fall below standard requirements.

2. A3 and Acm Lines: The Process "Watershed" for Hypo-eutectoid and Hyper-eutectoid Steels

  • A3 Line (GS Line): The temperature boundary where ferrite completely dissolves into austenite during the heating of hypo-eutectoid steel (carbon content < 0.77%).
  • Acm Line (ES Line): The temperature boundary where cementite completely dissolves into austenite during the heating of hyper-eutectoid steel (carbon content > 0.77%).

Engineering Significance—Why Can't They Be Treated Equally?

For hypo-eutectoid steels (such as WCB cast steel and A105 forged steel), the heating temperature for normalizing or full annealing should be controlled at 30–50°C above Ac3. This window ensures that all ferrite transforms into austenite. Subsequent cooling in air (normalizing) or furnace cooling (annealing) allows the austenite to re-transform into a refined ferrite + pearlite microstructure, achieving grain refinement and internal stress relief.

For hyper-eutectoid steels, the rules are entirely different:

  • Normalizing temperature must reach 30–50°C above Accm. The primary purpose of normalizing is to eliminate the network cementite precipitated under as-cast or as-forged conditions—this hard and brittle phase severely reduces the impact toughness of the valve. Only by raising the temperature above Accm can the network cementite dissolve fully into austenite, and subsequent air cooling "locks in" this elimination effect.
  • Annealing/Quenching temperature should be controlled at 30–50°C above Ac1 (i.e., the dual-phase region) rather than above Accm. If heated into the single-phase austenite region, network cementite will precipitate again during slow cooling, worsening performance; meanwhile, the amount of retained austenite after quenching will increase, leading to a drop in hardness.

3. Performance Positioning of Ferrite + Pearlite Matrix

The target room-temperature microstructure for carbon steel valves (carbon content < 2.14%) is ferrite + pearlite. The engineering considerations for this combination are:

  • Pearlite (a lamellar mixture of ferrite and cementite) provides structural strength, with a tensile strength reaching 770–870 MPa, much higher than pure ferrite's 250–300 MPa.
  • Ferrite provides ductility and toughness, enabling the valve to resist cold embrittlement fractures under low-temperature operating conditions. However, it must be noted that all steels with a ferrite-pearlite matrix exhibit low-temperature cold brittleness. When the temperature drops below a certain critical threshold, the impact energy drops sharply. Therefore, valve design must clearly define a safety margin between the minimum service temperature and the material's ductile-to-brittle transition temperature (DBTT).

4. Summary

The three critical lines of the iron-carbon phase diagram—A1 (heat treatment baseline), A3, and Acm (the watershed for normalizing/annealing upper temperature limits)—together constitute the temperature coordinate system for valve thermal processing. The core principle in actual production is to determine which line to use as the heating baseline based on the steel's carbon content, while considering the phase transformation point shifts caused by actual heating/cooling rates, ultimately striking a balance among refining grains, eliminating stress, and avoiding brittle phase precipitation.
